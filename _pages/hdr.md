### Summary

In this Habilitation à Diriger des Recherches, I present a synthesis of my work on the cosmological large-scale structure. Focusing on the synergy between X-ray and optical observations, I aim to constrain cosmological models and unravel the physics of galaxy formation. My research bridges the gap between theoretical models of dark matter halos and the complex baryonic physics observed in the real universe, leveraging data from major surveys such as eROSITA, DESI, SDSS, and the Legacy Surveys. In Chapter 1, I begin by establishing the theoretical framework for large-scale structure analysis, reviewing the standard Lambda Cold Dark Matter model and the primary probes used to test it, and distinguishing between geometric measurements—derived from standard rulers and candles—and growth-of-structure indicators such as clustering and weak lensing. Detailing the statistical methodology I employ, I describe the estimation of two-point correlation functions, galaxy-galaxy lensing, and the analytical Halo Occupation Distribution models that link observed galaxies to their underlying dark matter halos. Furthermore, I provide a panoramic overview of current and future multi-wavelength surveys spanning the radio to gamma-ray bands. Highlighting the unique capabilities of X-ray and optical instruments, I explore their role in mapping the cosmic web.

The core scientific contributions (Chapter 2) focus on three main areas: the role of AGN within the cosmic web, the properties of hot gas in Milky Way–mass haloes, and the cross-correlation between galaxies and X-ray emission. First, I analyze the large-scale environment of Active Galactic Nuclei selected across different wavelengths, including X-ray, optical, infrared, and radio. By combining clustering and lensing data, I demonstrate that AGN activity is primarily regulated by internal processes within the host galaxy. The large-scale environment appears to play a lesser role. Furthermore, I confirm a positive correlation between AGN clustering bias and X-ray luminosity.

Second, the detection and characterization of extended X-ray emission from the circumgalactic medium (CGM) around Milky Way–mass galaxies was carried out through the supervised thesis of Yi Zhang, representing a significant observational breakthrough. In this work, we stacked X-ray surface brightness profiles to measure the hot circumgalactic medium out to the virial radius. We established scaling relations between X-ray luminosity, stellar mass, and halo mass. A pivotal result is that star-forming and quiescent galaxies show different X-ray luminosities at fixed stellar mass. However, they have identical luminosities at fixed halo mass. This finding implies that halo mass, not instantaneous star formation, governs the thermodynamic state of the circumgalactic medium. It also supports a bimodal stellar-to-halo mass relation. The development of hydrodynamic modeling frameworks using IllustrisTNG simulations was led by PhD student Soumya Shreeram. This provided essential theoretical tools for interpreting observational measurements of the hot circumgalactic medium. Her efforts allowed us to disentangle the contributions from point sources such as AGN and X-ray binaries, hot gas in the circumgalactic medium, and satellite or host halo emission. Her analysis showed that the circumgalactic medium dominates the signal at intermediate scales for massive galaxies. This provides a powerful new probe of the gas-galaxy connection.

Third, I introduce a novel cross-correlation method between galaxy positions and the full soft X-ray photon field without masking sources, a technique I developed to maximize signal-to-noise and unify the interpretation of galaxies, hot gas, and point sources.

In the third chapter, I outline a roadmap for the next decade of cosmological research. I argue that to fully exploit the statistical power of upcoming surveys, we must move beyond traditional summary statistics and small-scale cuts. I propose a research project to develop a flexible, physical, multi-wavelength model. This model will self-consistently predict the distribution of gas, galaxies, and supermassive black holes within the dark matter cosmic web. By integrating this model into full-field inference algorithms, I aim to extract cosmological information from nonlinear scales. This approach addresses the roles of baryonic feedback, AGN physics, and the gas-galaxy-AGN connection in shaping the universe.

This HDR demonstrates my commitment to advancing our understanding of the baryonic universe. I combine cutting-edge observational data with robust theoretical modeling and simulation-based forward modeling. This work lays a solid foundation for future full-field, multi-wavelength cosmological analyses that are essential for precision cosmology in the coming decade. It shows my capacity to lead, structure, and synthesize research at the interface between observational cosmology, galaxy formation, and the statistical analysis of large surveys. This is set within a resolutely multi-wavelength context. A central aspect of this HDR is the strong integration of PhD supervision and mentoring into the research program. The scientific results presented are deeply intertwined with the supervision of doctoral students, whose work has been instrumental in advancing this topic.
